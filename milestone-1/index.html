One problem · every necessary concept

Make this image move.

The photograph gives us one moment. A video demands a future that was never recorded.

Place a photograph on the table.

In it, a golden dog stands in a field. Its mouth is open. One front paw is slightly raised. The grass behind it is soft with late-afternoon light.

Now imagine that someone slides a note beside the photograph:

Make the dog run through the field in slow motion while the camera follows alongside it.

Read the sentence once more, but this time do not picture the finished video. Pause at the photograph.

Where is the dog's next footstep?

Where is the side of its body that the camera cannot currently see? What shape will its ears take when air catches them? Which grass will bend under its paws? If the camera moves to the right, what should appear at the left edge of the frame? How far away is the tree near the horizon—and should it slide slowly or quickly as the viewpoint changes?

None of those answers is stored somewhere behind the photograph. The image is not a paused video waiting to be resumed. It is evidence from one instant.

To make it move, a machine must construct moments that were never recorded.

That is our way into Generative AI. We will not begin with a catalogue of architectures. We will begin with the missing future and earn each piece of machinery only when the problem demands it.

1. A photograph is rich—and radically incomplete

The image gives us more than a blank canvas. It constrains what a sensible continuation can be.

The dog has a particular coat colour, face, size and pose. The sunlight comes from a particular direction. The horizon sits at a certain height. The dog is in front of some parts of the field and behind others. Perspective gives clues about distance. Shadows give clues about shape.

These are promises the future video should keep.

But notice the wording: the image gives clues. It does not arrive with a neat inventory saying “dog,” “grass,” “three metres from the camera,” or “left ear hidden by perspective.” The file contains numbers describing colour and brightness at locations in a grid. A human—or a model—must turn those measurements into useful structure.

The photograph also leaves enormous gaps:

  • We cannot see the far side of the dog.
  • We do not know what lies just outside the frame.
  • We know the current pose, but not the next one.
  • We have clues about depth, but not a complete 3D model.
  • We have one instant, so we cannot directly measure motion.
  • We do not know whether the camera will remain fixed or move.

Try a small experiment. Imagine three five-second films beginning from this exact photograph.

In the first, the dog runs toward the camera. In the second, it turns and races to the right while the camera follows. In the third, it stays still while wind crosses the field.

All three can agree perfectly with the starting image. The photograph alone cannot tell us which future is “the” correct one.

The instruction removes some possibilities. “Runs” makes the still dog less suitable. “Through the field” rules out a sudden cut to a living room. “The camera follows alongside” favours a travelling viewpoint. “Slow motion” changes how movement should unfold across frames.

Yet the instruction still does not specify every joint angle, camera coordinate, shadow, or blade of grass. It narrows the future without completing it.

The first mental model: constrained imagination

Image-to-video generation lives between two bad extremes.

At one extreme, the system protects the input so fiercely that it repeats the photograph sixteen times. Identity is perfect. Nothing drifts. Nothing moves either.

At the other extreme, the system creates freely. The dog gallops, the camera swoops, and the field changes beautifully—but the dog's face becomes another face, the collar appears and disappears, and the horizon jumps.

The useful region lies between them:

Preserve what the evidence commits us to. Invent what the requested future requires.

That sentence is more important than any model name in this chapter.

Why attractive frames can still make a terrible video

Suppose we generate five beautiful pictures of a running dog. Viewed separately, each could win an image contest. Played in sequence, they may fail immediately.

Frame one gives the dog a red collar. Frame two loses it. Frame three shortens the muzzle. Frame four moves a fence post. Frame five changes the direction of the light.

The eye is unusually sensitive to this kind of disagreement. A small inconsistency that hides inside a single image becomes a flash when it changes from frame to frame.

Video therefore adds a new obligation: persistence. The future must not merely look plausible at each instant; the instants must belong to the same unfolding scene.

We have already uncovered several later parts of the stack—identity, motion, camera, hidden surfaces, time—without naming an architecture. That is the method of this series: let the failure reveal the missing capability.

2. A compact equation for “many futures”

We can now introduce one piece of mathematics because the idea already exists in words.

P(V | I, c)

Read it aloud:

Given image I and conditions c, how plausible is a possible video V?

I is our dog photograph. The conditions c may include the instruction, desired duration, camera path, pose, audio, style, or other controls. V is one candidate video.

The expression does not say that the model knows the real future. There may be no real future; the photograph could be staged. It says that some continuations fit the evidence and conditions better than others.

Imagine placing possible videos on a giant landscape. Videos that change the dog into a bicycle sit in a very low region. Videos in which the dog remains the same and begins running occupy higher ground. The instruction reshapes that landscape: a sideways tracking shot rises; a fixed camera becomes less favoured.

Generation will eventually mean finding one good route through this landscape. We are not ready to do that yet. First we must ask how the machine acquires any sense of what “good” looks like.

Keep two journeys separate

From here onward, we will move between two timelines.

In the learning journey, many earlier examples, objectives, and corrections shape a model. This is where loss, backpropagation, optimization, data, and scale belong.

In the request journey, that trained model receives our particular photograph and instruction. This is where the dog becomes visual representations, the sentence becomes language representations, and a possible video is eventually generated.

HOW THE MACHINE WAS BUILT              HOW OUR REQUEST MOVES THROUGH IT

objectives and loss                    this instruction
backpropagation                        encoders and representations
optimization                           generation and decoding
        ↓                                      ↓

The two journeys meet in the trained parameters, but they are not the same event. Our photograph is evidence for the request; it is not the entire education of the machine.

3. Why we cannot animate the world with a giant rulebook

The most natural first idea is also the one worth taking seriously: write rules.

Start with a simple rule: move the dog ten pixels to the right in each frame.

The result does not look like running. It looks like a cardboard cutout sliding across the grass.

So we add rules. Rotate the legs. Move the front paws forward and the rear paws backward. Raise and lower the body. Make the ears bounce. Shift the shadow. Change the size of the dog as the camera moves.

Now the paws slide because they do not remain planted during contact. The joints bend in impossible directions. The fur acts like a rigid shell. Grass that should be hidden remains visible through the legs. The far side of the body never appears. The background moves at one speed even though nearby grass should shift faster than the distant hills.

Each repair creates three more cases. A small dog runs differently from a large dog. A dog turning toward the camera projects a different shape from one moving sideways. Wet fur behaves differently from dry fur. A handheld camera behaves differently from a dolly.

There is nothing foolish about rules. Physics engines, animation rigs and graphics pipelines use them successfully when the world has been explicitly modelled. Our difficulty is that a random photograph does not arrive with a skeleton, depth map, material description, lighting setup, and 3D scene hidden behind it.

We need machinery that can extract reusable regularities from examples. We have moved, briefly, from the request journey to the question of how such machinery could be built.

Learning as compressed experience

Imagine an apprentice animator who has watched thousands of animals move. The apprentice has not memorized a verbal rule for every paw. Instead, practice has changed what they notice. They expect weight to shift before a leap. They recognize that a planted foot should not slide. They anticipate how perspective changes as a body turns.

Machine learning is far less human than this apprentice, but the comparison gives us one useful idea: examples can shape behaviour without every behaviour being written as a separate rule.

The model contains adjustable numbers called parameters or weights. During training, those numbers are changed until the system becomes better at a chosen task.

No individual weight means “golden fur” or “left paw.” Useful behaviour is distributed across many interactions. This matters because phrases such as “the model learned dogs” can create the false picture of a tidy dog file stored inside the network.

Where AI, machine learning, and deep learning fit

What we have just described has a name: machine learning. This gives us one useful moment to locate ourselves on the larger AI map. The map is orientation, not a new destination.

Artificial intelligence is the broad ambition: build machines that perform abilities we associate with intelligence—recognizing, predicting, planning, creating, or acting.

Machine learning is one route toward that ambition. Instead of writing every behaviour directly, we choose a learning setup and allow examples to shape the behaviour.

Deep learning is a family of machine-learning methods built from neural networks with many stages of learned transformation. “Deep” does not mean thoughtful or conscious. It refers to layers of computation.

A neural network is the adjustable numerical machinery inside that family. Its name was historically inspired by brains, but a modern artificial neuron is a mathematical operation, not a tiny biological cell with feelings or intention.

A generative model is defined by what we ask the learned system to do: produce new samples compatible with patterns and conditions. Text models generate token sequences. Image models generate visual content. Our image-to-video model must generate a sequence of frames while remaining constrained by an image and instruction.

The nesting looks like this:

Artificial intelligence — the broad goal
└── Machine learning — behaviour shaped by data
    └── Deep learning — multilayer neural networks
        └── Generative modelling — creating compatible new samples
            └── Image-to-video — creating a video from an image and controls

These are not rival brands. Saying “we use deep learning instead of neural networks” is like saying “we use a novel instead of sentences.” One level is built from the other.

Learning is useful only if it travels beyond the examples

Imagine a model that reproduces every training clip perfectly but becomes helpless when shown a dog it has never seen. It has stored answers without acquiring a reusable ability.

The goal is generalization: patterns learned from earlier examples should remain useful for a new combination of dog, field, pose, instruction, and camera.

Generalization is not guaranteed. It depends on what the data covers, what the objective rewards, how much capacity the model has, and how similar the new situation is to its experience. A model trained mostly on steady daylight clips may struggle with a fast handheld night scene. A dataset with narrow cultural or visual coverage can create blind spots. A model can also learn shortcuts—background clues that correlate with an action in the data but do not cause it.

This is why “the training loss went down” is evidence of progress, not proof that the system understands the world.

One training step, slowed down

Take one short training clip in which a dog runs across a field. Hide or corrupt part of the information and ask the model to make a prediction relevant to its training objective.

Its first attempt is poor. Perhaps the predicted representation places the front paw too high and blurs the edge of the body.

Four things now happen:

  1. The attempt is compared with a target.
  2. A loss function turns selected differences into a number.
  3. Backpropagation calculates how each adjustable parameter contributed to that loss.
  4. An optimizer nudges the parameters in directions expected to reduce future loss.

Then the process repeats—with another corruption, another frame, another clip, another dog, another camera.

Loss is a ruler, not a teacher with judgment

The word loss can sound as though the machine feels disappointment. It does not. Loss is a measurement chosen by designers.

That measurement is powerful precisely because it is specific. It may measure error in predicted noise, reconstruction, alignment, or another training target. But specificity also creates a limitation: the model becomes better at what the objective rewards, not automatically at everything people care about.

A video can achieve a good numerical score and still feel wrong. The score may not fully capture whether the dog's personality appears unchanged, whether the motion has convincing weight, or whether a subtle camera move feels natural.

This is one reason evaluation remains difficult later in the stack. “Looks right to a person” is not one simple quantity.

Backpropagation does not decide what matters

Backpropagation often receives mystical explanations. Its role is narrower and more mechanical.

Many parameters helped produce the attempt. The loss tells us how wrong the selected outcome was. Backpropagation uses the chain rule to carry that information backward through the calculations, estimating how a tiny change in each parameter would affect the loss.

It assigns numerical responsibility. It does not choose the values of the project. The objective and data did that.

The optimizer is cautious for a reason

If we changed every parameter dramatically after one bad paw, the model might improve that example and damage a thousand others. Optimizers therefore take controlled steps, combining gradient information across training examples and time.

Learning emerges from an enormous number of small corrections, not from a single moment of understanding.

Training is not what happens after you press Generate

This distinction is easy to lose because both stages use the same model.

Training is the long period during which examples, objectives, backpropagation, and optimization shape the parameters. It can consume enormous datasets and distributed computing.

Inference begins when we provide our dog photograph and instruction to the already-trained system. The model uses what its parameters have acquired. It does not relearn the meaning of dogs from our single picture.

During training During our request
many examples shape parameters one new image constrains a result
loss and gradients guide updates the main model weights usually remain fixed
broad reusable patterns are acquired those patterns are applied to this scene
hours, days, or months may be spent a result is expected in practical time

The image is evidence for inference, not the complete education of the model.

But names and training loops do not move our dog. We have answered how the machinery could acquire useful behaviour. We have not answered what happens when this photograph enters it.

4. The user sees a dog; the machine receives arrays

We have explained how useful machinery can be learned. We still have not explained what enters it.

Zoom into the photograph until the dog's eye disappears into coloured squares. Each square records measurements—commonly red, green, and blue channel values. A model does not begin with “eye,” “dog,” or even “object.”

Likewise, the instruction does not arrive as intention. A tokenizer first divides it into processable pieces. Depending on the tokenizer, a piece may be a word, part of a word, punctuation, or whitespace pattern. Each token is represented by an identifier.

Pixels and token IDs are raw ingredients. To guide a video, they must become representations in which useful relationships are easier to express.

Why an encoder is more than a file converter

An encoder is a learned transformation. It changes raw input into features that later machinery can use.

For the image, useful features may carry evidence about edges, texture, parts, shapes, objects, depth, composition, and relationships. These are not clean human labels stored in separate boxes. They are distributed numerical patterns that become useful because of how the whole system was trained.

For the sentence, useful features must depend on context. “Run” in “the dog will run” is not quite the same as “the colours run in the rain.” “Alongside” describes a spatial relationship. “Slow motion” changes temporal presentation rather than the dog's identity.

The encoder's job is not merely to shrink data. It makes distinctions that later stages need.

Two ways to begin seeing an image

We can now name two common visual routes because we know why a route is necessary.

A convolutional neural network (CNN) uses learned filters that move across local neighbourhoods. The same filter can notice a pattern wherever it appears. Layers combine small patterns into larger ones. This gives CNNs a useful bias toward locality: nearby pixels often belong together.

A Vision Transformer (ViT) divides the image into patches and turns them into token-like participants. Later attention mechanisms allow patches to exchange information. A patch covering part of a paw can be interpreted using distant patches containing the body, ground, and surrounding scene.

“Small moving windows” and “tiles comparing notes” are helpful intuitions, but neither architecture literally sees named parts the way we do. Both learn numerical representations whose usefulness depends on data and objectives.

Tokens are pieces; embeddings are learned coordinates

A token is a unit presented to the model. An embedding is a vector—a list of numbers—that represents a token or input piece in a learned space.

Why use a vector instead of a dictionary definition?

Because later computation needs relationships. In a useful embedding space, representations can carry patterns of similarity, difference, context, and role. The embedding for “dog” does not need to contain an English sentence defining a dog. It needs to interact usefully with other representations and with what the model sees.

Context matters. The text encoder should produce a representation of “runs” influenced by “dog,” “field,” and “slow motion.” The same token in another sentence can receive a different contextual representation.

The two languages must meet

We now have a visual representation and a language representation. That alone does not guarantee that the phrase “the dog” points toward the correct regions of the image.

Multimodal alignment is the learned compatibility that lets representations from different modalities participate in shared meaning. During training, related images and text can be pulled into useful correspondence while unrelated pairs are distinguished.

For our request, alignment must support several connections:

  • “dog” should refer to the animal, not the field;
  • “runs” should affect its motion, not merely add blur;
  • “slow motion” should influence temporal unfolding;
  • “camera follows alongside” should guide viewpoint change;
  • the source image should keep the particular dog's appearance in control.

Weak alignment produces a familiar failure: the system understands every ingredient separately but combines them incorrectly.

Optional controls are not decorative extras

A modern video system may accept more than image and text:

  • a pose sequence can constrain body configuration;
  • depth can provide rough geometry;
  • edges or segmentation can preserve structure;
  • audio can influence timing or events;
  • a camera path can constrain viewpoint;
  • duration and frame rate define the temporal canvas.

Each signal needs a representation and a route into the model. Adding a modality is not just adding another upload button; it creates a new coordination problem.

Return to the field. Somewhere inside these vectors, “dog” can now interact differently from “field,” and “camera follows alongside” can remain distinct from “dog runs.” The request is no longer merely pixels beside token IDs.

But we still have not generated a single frame.

WHAT WE NOW HAVE
Visual representations of the scene and contextual representations of the instruction, with enough learned alignment for them to refer to a shared request.

WHAT WE STILL DO NOT HAVE
A practical place to construct an entire video.

Do we really want to invent every future directly among tens of millions of raw pixel values?

5. Forty-four million numbers are a clumsy workshop

Consider a modest video: 16 frames, each 1280 pixels wide and 720 pixels high, with three colour channels.

16 × 1280 × 720 × 3 = 44,236,800

That is more than forty-four million colour values for less than a second of video at 16 frames per second. A longer or higher-resolution clip quickly multiplies the burden.

More importantly, raw pixels are not always the most useful level at which to make every decision. If the dog moves slightly, millions of pixel values change even though the underlying event is simple: the same dog changed pose and position.

The generator needs a more compact workshop.

A latent space is a working representation

A latent representation is compressed, learned, and hidden from direct view. It keeps patterns useful to the model while discarding some literal detail.

The popular “suitcase” analogy helps at first: when travelling, we pack what we expect to need and leave the room behind. But a latent is not a suitcase with separate compartments labelled DOG, FIELD, and LIGHT. Information is distributed. A change in one numerical dimension may affect several visible qualities.

A better second analogy is an architect's model. It is smaller than the building and omits wallpaper fibres, yet it preserves relationships useful for planning. The danger is equally clear: if the model omits a staircase, no amount of careful planning inside the model can recover its exact shape later.

The autoencoder learns what to keep

An autoencoder has two cooperating parts:

pixels → encoder → latent representation → decoder → reconstructed pixels

During training, the encoder compresses an input and the decoder tries to reconstruct it. Reconstruction error pressures the latent to retain useful information.

The bottleneck matters. If the latent were as large and unconstrained as the input, the system could simply copy. Compression forces choices.

For image-to-video, we can encode visible frames into latents, perform expensive generative work in that smaller space, and decode the result back into frames. This is one reason latent generative systems can be dramatically more practical than systems operating at full pixel resolution throughout.

Compression is a bargain, not a free lunch

More compression makes later computation cheaper. It also creates more opportunities to lose information.

Imagine that the dog's collar contains tiny engraved letters. If the encoder discards them, the decoder cannot faithfully restore them. It may invent letter-like marks because those look plausible. The result can be visually convincing and factually wrong.

This gives us a general lesson that will return throughout Generative AI:

A representation optimized for plausible reconstruction is not automatically a reliable database of facts.

Latents can lose fine text, exact counts, subtle geometry, and rare details. They are useful models for generation, not guaranteed replicas of the world.

Why a variational autoencoder organizes the workshop

A plain autoencoder may reconstruct well while leaving its latent space irregular. Imagine that familiar images occupy isolated islands with empty, unreliable water between them. Moving from one island to another could pass through regions the decoder has never learned to handle.

A variational autoencoder (VAE) adds pressure for a smoother, more organized latent distribution. Rather than encoding an input as only one rigid point, the encoder commonly describes a small distribution using a mean and spread. Training balances two desires:

  1. preserve enough information to reconstruct the input;
  2. keep the latent space regular enough to sample and navigate.

Push too hard on reconstruction and the space may remain awkward. Push too hard on regularity and fine details may wash away.

We do not need the full derivation yet. The mental model is enough: reconstruction makes the latent useful; regularization makes it navigable.

Where is time in this representation?

One image latent represents one moment. A video representation must also locate information in time.

Some systems compress individual frames and let later temporal machinery connect them. Others use video-aware encoders that compress small blocks across space and time. Either way, position matters. The system needs ways to distinguish left from right, top from bottom, and earlier from later.

Without positional information, the same collection of patches could be rearranged without the model knowing which arrangement we meant.

We now possess a compact scene representation. But possessing pieces is not the same as knowing how they should influence one another.

Return once more to the dog's coat. In pixel space it was millions of colour measurements. In the latent it is no longer a literal patch of golden pixels, yet enough structure must survive for the decoder to reconstruct the same animal. If that structure is weak, the coat, face, collar, or body proportions may drift when the future is generated.

6. Stop and rebuild the machine

Before we add another technical term, let us return to the photograph.

The user supplies a dog image and an instruction. Those inputs meet machinery shaped earlier by training.

The image encoder turns pixels into useful visual evidence. The text encoder turns tokenized language into contextual embeddings. Optional controls follow their own routes. A latent encoder places visual information into a smaller working space.

Two stories are happening at different times:

LEARNING STORY
examples + objectives
        ↓
loss → backpropagation → optimizer
        ↓
trained parameters

REQUEST STORY
new image + instruction
        ↓
encoders
        ↓
visual latent + language embeddings

Do not merge these stories. The user's image enters the request story. It is not the dataset from which the whole model learns.

Read failures backward through the machine

A useful mental model should help when the result goes wrong. Consider what different failures suggest—not as perfect diagnoses, but as places to investigate.

What we observe in the generated clip Questions it raises
The dog slowly becomes a different dog Was identity represented strongly enough? Did conditioning remain influential through time?
The prompt says “run,” but the dog barely moves Did language align with the correct subject? Did later motion-generation machinery follow the condition?
The dog moves, but legs stretch or multiply Has the system learned robust body and motion regularities? Can its spatial-temporal representation preserve structure?
The background bends as the camera travels Does the system have enough depth, geometry, occlusion, and camera consistency?
Tiny collar lettering mutates Was the detail lost during compression, or plausibly reconstructed rather than faithfully preserved?
Each frame looks excellent, but the clip flickers Are temporal relationships and persistence too weak?

Several components can contribute to one failure. The point is not to assign blame from appearance alone. The point is to replace “AI video is weird” with informed questions about evidence, representation, conditioning, generation, time, and decoding.

7. The missing capability is now visible

Inside the machine we have visual regions, language embeddings, positions, and perhaps camera or pose controls.

But which visual regions should listen to the word “dog”? How should “slow motion” influence time without changing identity? How can a patch containing one paw use information from the body several patches away? How should the camera instruction affect the background differently from the animal?

The pieces need selective communication.

That need will introduce attention, cross-attention, Transformers, and temporal attention in the next milestone. Notice how different this feels from beginning with the sentence “Transformers use attention.” We now have a reason to want attention before receiving its name.

8. Test the mental model—not the vocabulary

Close the article and try to explain the following to another person:

  1. Why is the photograph evidence rather than a paused future?
  2. Give two different videos that could honestly begin from the same image.
  3. Why does a sliding cardboard cutout expose the limit of hand-written rules?
  4. What role belongs to loss, and what role belongs to backpropagation?
  5. Why is training different from the moment the user presses Generate?
  6. What is the difference between a token and an embedding?
  7. Why must image and language representations be aligned?
  8. Why is a latent useful, and what can compression destroy?
  9. What is still missing after Milestone 1?

If the answer to the final question is “a way for the right pieces to find and influence one another,” the next machinery has been earned.

Milestone 1 in one sentence

Before an image-to-video system can invent motion, learned encoders must turn partial visual evidence and human instructions into compact, compatible internal representations—and the next challenge is teaching those representations to coordinate.

We began with a photograph that could not move. We end with something more precise than a list of AI terms: a partial machine, a set of responsibilities, and a clearly visible gap.

The dog is still standing in the field.

Now we know why making it take the first believable step is such a profound engineering problem.
